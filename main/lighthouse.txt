The Lighthouse at Marrow Point

Every evening, just before the sun touched the water, Tomas climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room. He did not count them anymore. His knees did that for him, one small complaint at a time, and by the top they had said everything they had to say.

The lamp was older than he was, and so was the brass rail that ran around it. He polished both, because a lighthouse that is not polished feels, to the man who keeps it, like a promise half kept. When the glass was clear and the wick was trimmed, he lit the lamp and stood back while the great lens woke up and began to turn.

For the first hour the light swept over empty water. Tomas liked that hour best. The sea went from silver to slate to a dark so complete that the beam seemed to be the only solid thing in the world, a long pale arm laid across the waves, pointing at nothing in particular and reassuring everyone anyway.

That night a small boat appeared at the edge of the beam. It was a fishing boat with a patched sail, and it was far too close to the rocks. Tomas watched it through the glass, his hand tight on the rail. He could not shout across that distance, and he could not row out in time. All he could do was what he had always done, which was to keep the light turning, steady and slow, and trust that somebody out there was looking at it.

The boat turned. It was a slow, careful turn, the kind a tired person makes when they have decided to trust something. It slid past the rocks with perhaps ten feet to spare and disappeared into the dark beyond the point, toward the harbor and the lamps of the town.

Tomas let out a breath he felt he had been holding for years. He wiped the glass, though it did not need it, and sat down on the old stool by the window. Down below, the sea went on washing the stones. Up here, the light went on turning.

In the morning he would climb down the one hundred and twelve steps, make tea, and sleep. In the evening he would climb them again. It was not much of a life, some people said. Tomas had never found a good answer to them, except that last night, for a few minutes, a stranger had been safe because he was there.

That seemed to him a fair answer. He sat with it until the sky began to pale, and then he put out the lamp.
